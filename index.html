The torque curve is built through your four peak points, shaped by the induction type, and anchored so that power falls after its peak the way a real engine does. Boost is derived from the crank figure and displacement: a forced-induction engine's naturally aspirated baseline is calculated from its swept volume, and whatever pressure ratio is needed to reach your target output is reported as boost.

Launch. Launch control holds a chosen rpm and, on a boosted car, builds nearly full boost against the brakes by retarding ignition and running rich to drive the turbine. Brake boosting is the manual version — throttle against the brakes — and it is limited twice over: the brakes can only hold about 2,800 rpm, and without the ignition trickery the turbos barely spool, which is why owners measure one or two psi doing it against fifteen or more on launch control. Off the line with no launch at all, a turbo car starts at idle with nothing in the manifold and has to wait for the exhaust to build.

Shift points. Fastest mode seeds from the crossover rule — shift when the next gear's wheel torque exceeds this gear's — then refines against the simulation itself, which catches things the rule misses, like short-shifting first on a car that is already spinning its tyres.

Gear changes follow the gearbox and drive mode. A torque-converter automatic in Normal slips its clutches on over about a third of a second: drive torque sags to roughly 60% and recovers smoothly, so the car keeps pulling and the body barely moves. Sport and Track fill the clutches harder and quicker, and Drag Strip holds nearly full torque through a sub-0.1 s change that slams the driveline. A dual-clutch box hands over from one clutch to the other with little torque gap. A manual or single-clutch automated manual has to open the clutch, so torque drops to zero, the car coasts briefly, and the nose dips before the clutch bites again.

Two 0-60 figures are reported. The first is a true standing start; the second applies the one-foot rollout that drag strips and virtually every published road test use, because the timer only starts once the car has already moved a foot. That is worth roughly three tenths, so it matters which one you are comparing against — most phone timers and magazine figures are rollout numbers.

The 3D view extrudes each car's side profile into a solid body, so the silhouettes stay true to the real dimensions — the Challenger is still the longest with a flat roof, the Camaro still the lowest. Wheels spin at road speed, the body pitches on its springs, brake lights fire under deceleration and the driven wheels lay skid marks when they break traction.

The camera has three modes. Chase rides just behind and above the car you pick, Trackside watches from the side of the strip as they come past, and Wide pulls right back to take in the whole run. If you are following the leader and it crosses first, the camera holds at the stripe for the trailing car.

Sound is synthesised, not sampled. Firing frequency is rpm divided by sixty, times half the cylinder count, and each engine gets its own harmonic recipe: a cross-plane V8 carries a strong half-order component, which is exactly where its lopsided burble comes from, while a flat-plane V8 drops that and gains upper harmonics, so it howls instead. Superchargers get their own whine on top, locked to crank speed rather than to boost, because a blower is belt-driven: a roots pack sings a four-lobe rotor tone through a 2.4:1 pulley, and a centrifugal spins its impeller far faster for a much higher note that climbs with revs. Shifts cut the throttle and fire a short overrun crackle, boosted cars get a blow-off on the cut, and losing traction opens a resonant screech.

Rolling starts begin at the chosen speed in whichever gear makes the most wheel torque there, which is how a roll race actually works.
